Section 7.1 Text Fundamentals

Typography Basics

Introduction: Letters Are Everywhere

Typographic design is deeply embedded in our daily lives: from snack package labels to highway traffic signs. We are constantly surrounded by letterforms that provide perspective and context about brands, spaces, and ideas.

Typographic design is the art of crafting easy-to-read messages with aesthetically pleasing compositions. It does not require creating brand-new typefaces, but mastering how to combine existing ones professionally.

Typographic Styles

Ag

Serif (With Serifs / Feet)

Rooted in traditional calligraphy. They feature decorative strokes or "feet" at the ends of characters.

Classic & Formal
Best for: Printed books & long text
Ag

Sans Serif (Grotesque)

Lacks decorative terminal strokes. Clean, modern lines that are highly versatile across screens.

Modern & Clean
Best for: Digital screens & headlines
OAN

Geometric

Constructed from pure geometric forms. The "O" is a perfect circle with razor-sharp angles.

Pure Structure
Best for: Logos & futuristic design
Script

Script (Cursive)

Mimics handwriting and artistic penmanship. Can range from elegant calligraphy to casual sketches.

Handwritten
Use sparingly: Display titles or accents
Layout Warning!

Widows, Orphans, Runts & Visual Rivers

Common formatting mistakes that disrupt reading flow and clutter your page layout.

Widow Line

Error

The last line of a paragraph sits alone at the top of a new column or page.

Orphan Line

Error

The first line of a paragraph sits isolated at the bottom of a column or page.

Short Line (Runt)

Error

A single short word stranded on the last line of a paragraph, breaking page harmony.

Visual Rivers

Error

Unwanted vertical gaps running through justified body text due to poor word spacing.

Best Practices for Pairing Typefaces

1

Combine Sans + Serif

Creates a natural, harmonious contrast between modern and classic styles.

2

Avoid Similar Fonts

Using two similar typefaces causes visual conflict rather than clear contrast.

3

Match Historical Eras

Pick fonts sharing historical context but serving distinct hierarchy levels.

4

Simplicity First

Limit designs to maximum 2 typefaces and leverage font weights (bold, regular, italic).

Section 7.2 Order & Structure

Visual Hierarchy

174 Newspapers a Day Eye-opening Fact • Dr. Martin Hilbert

According to research by Dr. Martin Hilbert (University of California), we receive an overwhelming amount of information equivalent to 174 full newspapers every day (over 5 times the amount in 1986). Furthermore, 80% of that information is purely visual. Establishing a clear visual hierarchy is essential to guide viewers without cognitive fatigue.

Level 1

Primary Element (Headline)

The primary focal entry point. Captures immediate attention using large scale, strong contrast, or heavy weight.

MAIN HEADLINE Immediate Impact
Level 2

Subheadings & Sections

Organizes content into digestible chunks. Guides the audience sequentially through the core story arc.

Section Subtitle Intermediate Structure
Level 3

Body Text & Details

Contains detailed descriptions. Prioritizes comfortable readability with proper line height and comfortable font sizes (14px - 18px).

Section 7.3 Layout & Distribution

Visual Composition

Rule of Thirds

Divides your canvas into a 3x3 grid. Position key focal points along grid intersections for natural dynamism.

Negative Space (Air)

Empty space is not wasted space; it lets designs breathe, highlights focal points, and projects elegance.

Alignment & Grids

Visually ties elements together. Aligning edges creates clear structure, unity, and cohesion.

Balance & Weights

Distributes visual weights symmetrically or asymmetrically so no area feels cluttered or heavy.
